[2] HEADING « What 'the Beat Divides' Means »
[3] TEXT: Every piece of music has a steady pulse running through it. That pulse is called the **beat**. You have already worked with beats throughout this course: counting "1, 2, 3, 4" in 4/4, feeling the strong first beat in a bar of 3/4, and so on.

Now we are going to look a little more closely at what is happening *inside* each beat.
[4] CALLOUT (key): **The beat divides** means: each beat can be split into smaller, equal pieces. The number of pieces each beat splits into is one of the most important ideas in music theory, and it is the whole reason we have two families of time signature: **simple time** and **compound time**.
[5] TEXT: Think of the beat like a slice of cake. You can cut each slice into two equal pieces, or into three equal pieces. The cake is still the same size. But the feeling you get is very different depending on which cut you make. Music works the same way.

[18] HEADING « Simple Time Revisited: The Beat Splits in Two »
[19] TEXT: Before we meet anything new, let's pin down exactly what **simple time** means. The word "simple" here does not mean "easy". It means something very specific about how each beat behaves.
[20] CALLOUT (key): **Simple time** is any time signature in which every beat divides naturally into **two equal halves**. That is the whole definition. If the beat splits into two, the time is simple.
[21] TEXT: You already know 4/4 well, so let's use it as the example. In 4/4, the beat is a **crotchet** (quarter note). One crotchet lasts for one beat. [31] HEADING « All the Time Signatures You Already Know Are Simple »
[32] TEXT: Look at every time signature you have studied so far. Every single one is a simple time signature. Here they are together:
[33] TEXT: - 2/4, 3/4, 4/4
- 2/2, 3/2, 4/2
- 3/8
[34] TEXT: In each of these, the **top number** tells you how many beats there are in a bar. The **bottom number** tells you what kind of note gets one beat. And in every case, that beat note splits naturally into **two equal halves**. That is what makes them all simple.
[35] EXAMPLE: - **2/4**: 2 beats per bar, each beat is a crotchet. One crotchet splits into 2 quavers. Simple.
- **3/4**: 3 beats per bar, each beat is a crotchet. One crotchet splits into 2 quavers. Simple.
- **4/4**: 4 beats per bar, each beat is a crotchet. One crotchet splits into 2 quavers. Simple.
- **2/2**: 2 beats per bar, each beat is a minim (half note). One minim splits into 2 crotchets. Simple.
- **3/2**: 3 beats per bar, each beat is a minim. One minim splits into 2 crotchets. Simple.
- **4/2**: 4 beats per bar, each beat is a minim. One minim splits into 2 crotchets. Simple.
- **3/8**: 3 beats per bar, each beat is a quaver (eighth note). One quaver splits into 2 semiquavers (sixteenth notes). Simple.
[36] CALLOUT (tip): A quick way to spot simple time: look at the top number of the time signature. If it is **2, 3, or 4**, the time signature is almost certainly simple. Those numbers count the beats directly, and simple-time beats always split into two.
[37] CALLOUT (note): You might wonder: what if the beat splits into **three** instead of two? That is exactly the question the next section answers. A time signature where every beat splits into three equal parts is called **compound time**, and it looks quite different on the page.

[39] HEADING « Compound Time: The Beat Splits in Three »
[40] TEXT: In the last section you saw that simple time slices every beat into two equal halves. Now meet the other main family: **compound time**.

In compound time, every beat divides into exactly **three** equal parts, not two. That triple split is the one defining feature of compound time. Everything else follows from it.
[41] CALLOUT (key): **Compound time:** a time signature in which every beat divides naturally into three equal parts.
[42] TEXT: Three-part beats produce a very distinctive sound. Instead of a straight, even pulse, the music develops a gentle rolling quality, sometimes described as a **lilt**. Think of a lullaby, a boat rocking on water, or a Irish jig. That swaying, forward-leaning feeling almost always comes from beats that split into three.

[59] HEADING « Reading Compound Time Signatures: 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 »
[60] TEXT: You have already met time signatures like 4/4 and 3/4, where the beat is a crotchet (a quarter note). Compound time signatures look a little different at first glance, but once you know the two-step reading trick, they make perfect sense.
[61] TEXT: The three main compound time signatures are:
- 6/8
- 9/8
- 12/8

All three share the same bottom number: **8**. That 8 means the written note value is a **quaver** (an eighth note, the note with one flag on its stem). The top number then tells you how many of those quavers fill one bar.
[62] CALLOUT (key): **The two numbers, explained plainly**

- **Bottom number (8):** the written note value is a quaver.
- **Top number (6, 9 or 12):** how many quavers fit in one bar.
[63] TEXT: So 6/8 means "six quavers per bar", 9/8 means "nine quavers per bar", and 12/8 means "twelve quavers per bar". You could fill each bar by writing that many quavers in a row, and the bar would be exactly full.

[67] HEADING « Finding the Real Beats: Divide the Top Number by 3 »
[68] TEXT: Here is the key insight. In compound time, every beat is a **dotted crotchet** (a dotted quarter note). A dotted crotchet lasts for three quavers. So to find how many beats are actually in a bar, you divide the top number by 3.
[69] CALLOUT (key): **The golden rule for compound time signatures:**

Top number divided by 3 = number of dotted-crotchet beats per bar.

- 6 divided by 3 = **2 beats** (6/8)
- 9 divided by 3 = **3 beats** (9/8)
- 12 divided by 3 = **4 beats** (12/8)
[70] EXAMPLE: | Time signature | Quavers per bar | Divide by 3 | Beats per bar | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6/8 | 6 | 6 / 3 | 2 dotted-crotchet beats | Two big beats, each split into three |
| 9/8 | 9 | 9 / 3 | 3 dotted-crotchet beats | Three big beats, each split into three |
| 12/8 | 12 | 12 / 3 | 4 dotted-crotchet beats | Four big beats, each split into three |
[71] TEXT: Think of each dotted-crotchet beat as a little group of three quavers that belong together. The bar is not a stream of six, nine, or twelve equal ticks. It is two, three, or four **big beats**, each of which rocks gently into three smaller steps.

[92] HEADING « Counting and Grouping in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 »
[93] TEXT: In the previous section you learned that compound time signatures like 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 tell you how many **quavers** (eighth notes) fit in a bar. But when you actually play or count music in these time signatures, you do not count every quaver. You count the **beats**, and each beat is worth a **dotted crotchet** (a dotted quarter note), which lasts for three quavers.

So instead of counting "1-2-3-4-5-6" for every quaver in a 6/8 bar, you count just **two beats**. Each beat is then subdivided into three smaller pulses. This is what gives compound time its flowing, triplet-like feel.
[94] HEADING « How to Count Aloud »
[95] TEXT: Use the syllables **"1-and-a"** for each beat. The word "1" lands on the beat itself, "and" is the second quaver, and "a" is the third quaver. Then the next beat begins on "2", and so on.

Here is how that looks for each time signature:

- **6/8:** two beats per bar: "1-and-a  2-and-a"
- **9/8:** three beats per bar: "1-and-a  2-and-a  3-and-a"
- **12/8:** four beats per bar: "1-and-a  2-and-a  3-and-a  4-and-a"
[96] CALLOUT (tip): Say the syllables evenly and at the same speed every time. Each syllable is one quaver. Tap your foot on "1" and "2" (the beats), and say "and-a" between taps. This builds the feel of compound time in your body before your fingers ever touch the keys.
[97] EXAMPLE: Six quavers in a row fill one bar of 6/8. Count them like this:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Say: | **1** | and | a | **2** | and | a |

Tap your foot on "1" and "2" only. Everything else is a subdivision of those two beats.

[103] HEADING « Grouping Rules: How Notes Are Beamed »
[104] TEXT: In compound time, beaming (the thick lines that join quavers together) is used to show the beats clearly. The rule is simple:

**Beam quavers in groups of three to match each beat.**

This means a reader can see at a glance exactly where each beat starts and ends. Never beam across a beat boundary. Each group of three quavers belongs to exactly one beat.
[105] CALLOUT (key): **The standard beat note in compound time is the dotted crotchet.** It lasts three quavers, so it fills one complete beat on its own. When you see a dotted crotchet, it takes up beats 1, 2, 3 or 4 all by itself, with no extra beaming needed.
[106] TEXT: You do not have to fill every beat with three separate quavers. Within one beat group you can also use:

- A **dotted crotchet** (one note lasting the whole beat)
- A **crotchet plus a quaver** (the crotchet lasts two quavers, the quaver lasts one)
- Three separate **quavers** beamed together

All three options fill exactly three quavers worth of time, so they all fit neatly into one beat.
[107] NOTATION caption=Bar 1: two dotted crotchets (one per beat). Bar 2: one dotted crotchet, then a crotchet and a quaver. Both bars are correct 6/8 groupings. | abc=X:1 M:6/8 L:1/8 K:C [M:6/8] C3 C D E |]
[108] CALLOUT (note): A crotchet lasts two quavers. When you pair a crotchet with a quaver inside the same beat, the crotchet comes first and the quaver follows. This is the only way round that works, because the longer note naturally sits at the start of the beat.

[131] HEADING « Dotted Notes, Ties and Rests in Compound Time »
[132] TEXT: You already know that in compound time each beat is a dotted note worth three quavers. That single fact shapes every rhythm you will read and write in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8. [133] HEADING « The dotted crotchet: one full beat »
[134] TEXT: In 6/8, each beat lasts a dotted crotchet (three quavers). A dotted crotchet is a crotchet with a small dot beside it. The dot adds half the crotchet's value, so the total is one-and-a-half crotchet beats, which equals exactly three quavers.

When you see a dotted crotchet in 6/8, think: **one full beat**. Nothing more to count.

[147] HEADING « Rests in compound time: keeping the beat clear »
[148] TEXT: A rest is a moment of silence. In compound time the golden rule is the same as for notes: **each beat must be clearly visible**. You use rests in combinations that match the beat groupings, so a performer can always see where beat 1 and beat 2 fall.

The two rest values you will use most are:
- The **quaver rest**: lasts one quaver (one third of a beat in compound time)
- The **crotchet rest**: lasts two quavers (two thirds of a beat)
[149] CALLOUT (watch): **Avoid writing a single crotchet rest across the boundary between two beats.** That would blur where one beat ends and the next begins. Always group rests so that each beat can be seen separately.

[163] HEADING « Simple or Compound? How to Tell at a Glance »
[164] TEXT: You have now met both types of time. Let's bring it all together into one clear decision rule you can use whenever you pick up a piece of music.
[165] CALLOUT (key): **The one question to ask:** Is the top number of the time signature 2, 3, or 4? Or is it 6, 9, or 12?

- **2, 3, or 4 = Simple time.** The beat is the note shown by the bottom number.
- **6, 9, or 12 = Compound time.** Divide the top number by 3 to find the number of beats per bar. The beat is a dotted note worth three of the bottom-number notes.
[166] HEADING « The Simple Side »
[167] TEXT: In simple time the top number tells you directly how many beats are in each bar.

- **2/4**: 2 beats per bar, each beat is a quarter note.
- **3/4**: 3 beats per bar, each beat is a quarter note.
- **4/4**: 4 beats per bar, each beat is a quarter note.
- **2/2**: 2 beats per bar, each beat is a half note.
- **3/8**: 3 beats per bar, each beat is an eighth note.

Each of those beats naturally divides into **two equal halves**. That two-way split is the hallmark of simple time.
[168] PLAY caption= | notes=["C4","E4","G4","C4","E4","G4"] beats=[1,0.5,0.5,1,0.5,0.5] style=sequence
[169] HEADING « The Compound Side »
[170] TEXT: In compound time the top number does **not** tell you the number of beats directly. Instead, divide it by 3.

- **6/8**: 6 divided by 3 = **2 beats** per bar. Each beat is a dotted quarter note (worth three eighth notes).
- **9/8**: 9 divided by 3 = **3 beats** per bar. Each beat is a dotted quarter note.
- **12/8**: 12 divided by 3 = **4 beats** per bar. Each beat is a dotted quarter note.

Each of those beats naturally divides into **three equal parts**. That three-way split is the hallmark of compound time.

[190] HEADING « Playing connection »
[191] TEXT: Compound time is everywhere in the music you will play. A huge amount of folk music, lullabies, and Irish jigs sit in 6/8, and you will feel that gentle, rolling "one-and-a, two-and-a" pulse the moment you try them. Barcarolles (pieces that imitate the rocking of a boat) are almost always written in 6/8 or 12/8. Even within a single piece, a composer may switch between simple and compound time to change the feel of the music entirely. Once you can spot the time signature and ask yourself "does the beat split in two or in three?", you will know straight away how the music should flow under your fingers.
[192] HEADING « Key terms »
[193] TEXT: - **beat division**: the way each beat is split into smaller equal parts inside a bar
- **simple time**: a time signature in which each beat divides naturally into two equal parts (for example, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4)
- **compound time**: a time signature in which each beat divides naturally into three equal parts (for example, 6/8, 9/8, 12/8)
- **compound duple**: compound time with two beats per bar (6/8)
- **compound triple**: compound time with three beats per bar (9/8)
- **compound quadruple**: compound time with four beats per bar (12/8)
- **beat note**: in compound time, the dotted note that represents one full beat (a dotted quarter note in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8)
- **grouping**: the way notes and rests are beamed or arranged within a bar to show clearly where each beat falls
[194] HEADING « Summary »
[195] TEXT: - In **simple time**, every beat divides into **two** equal parts.
- In **compound time**, every beat divides into **three** equal parts.
- The most common compound time signatures are **6/8** (two beats per bar), **9/8** (three beats per bar), and **12/8** (four beats per bar).
- In these signatures the bottom number 8 means an eighth note, but the **real beat** is a **dotted quarter note** (worth three eighth notes).
- To tell simple from compound at a glance: if the top number is **2, 3 or 4**, it is simple; if it is **6, 9 or 12**, it is compound.
- Dotted notes, ties and rests follow the same rules in compound time as in simple time, but grouping must always show the **dotted-quarter beat** clearly.
- Hearing that natural "**one-and-a**" lilt is the quickest way to recognise compound time by ear.
