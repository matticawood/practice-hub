## 1. Don't run it through — you're practising your mistakes
- Here's the one that'll sting: every time you play a piece through and hit the same wrong note, you are not fixing it, you are *practising* it.
- Your brain reinforces whatever you repeat, and it can't tell right from wrong. Play a mistake ten times, you've rehearsed it ten times.
- I've nothing against running a piece through. Just save it for when you want to *enjoy* it, *perform* it, or *record* it. That's not practice.
- Instead: stop, isolate the exact spot, slow down until you can play it, then build it back up.
- **Studies show:** in motor learning, your brain reinforces whatever you repeat, right or wrong. So playing through a mistake practises it, it doesn't fix it.

## 2. Practising and playing are two different things
- That first point is really the tip of a bigger one: most self-taught players never actually *practise*, they only ever *play through* pieces. That's why they plateau.
- Practising is deliberate. You plan before you touch a key, you target the specific problem, you fix it.
- And fixing a problem now means you never have to fix it again, and it makes every *future* piece easier. You're learning a skill, not just a piece.
- **Analogy:** a golfer doesn't get better playing 18 holes every day. They hit the driving range and drill the same shot a hundred times. A tennis player drills one swing, they don't just play matches. Practising is your driving range.
- **Studies show:** focused, deliberate practice, where you concentrate and fix specific things, is what improves you, while playing on autopilot mostly reinforces what you already do. Research has also debunked the "10,000 hours" idea. It was never about the hours, it's about how you practise.

## 3. Getting the notes right is only half the job
- It's so easy to fall into the trap of pressing keys, just trying to get the right notes in the right order.
- The hard, important part is *how* you play them. The expression, the phrasing, the dynamics.
- (My opinion) I'd say the notes are only about 40 to 50 percent of it. The rest is how you play them.
- Music mirrors language. We phrase it, we get louder and softer to mark the moments that matter, exactly like when we speak.
- **Analogy:** imagine someone breaking up with you in a totally flat, monotone voice, no emotion at all. It would be bizarre. We're built to connect to emotion. All the right notes with no expression is exactly that: an emotional message delivered in a monotone.
- **Studies show:** a performer can communicate emotion to a listener about as accurately as the human *voice* can, using the same cues, timing and volume. So how you play carries as much feeling as your tone of voice.

## 4. Consistency beats intensity (and start smaller than you think)
- Ten minutes every day beats a marathon session once a week. The person who does a little daily will fly past the person who crams.
- Don't sit and play for hours early on, that's how you build the run-through habit from point 1. Learn *how* to practise first. You have to learn how to learn.
- Make it an identity: you're the type of person who practises daily. Start so small it's impossible to skip.
- **Studies show:** it takes on average around 66 days of daily repetition for something to become a habit, and much longer for some people. The popular "21 days" idea is a myth.

## 5. Theory isn't boring homework — it IS the music  [the one you teased]
- Theory is not a separate, dry subject. It's simply the description of what the music is already doing.
- All music is built from the same handful of things and the same natural phenomena, like frequencies. Once you get it, you start spotting the same patterns in every new piece.
- And putting a name to something helps your brain store it, so you recognise it instantly next time.
- **Analogy / studies show:** there's a famous study where chess masters could glance at a board and recall it perfectly, but only if it was a real game. Give them random pieces and they were no better than anyone. They weren't remembering pieces, they were remembering *patterns*. Theory is what lets you see the patterns instead of individual notes.

## 6. Master a few things deeply instead of collecting many
- Music honestly isn't that complicated. There are only so many notes, so many chords, and the concepts are easy enough to find.
- The gap isn't *knowing* a thing, it's having it completely automated so you don't have to think about it.
- So instead of half-learning 20 scales, learn one scale inside out. It makes every scale after it far easier anyway.
- **Studies show:** once a skill becomes automatic it stops taking up mental effort, which frees your brain to learn the next thing. Master one thing fully and everything after it gets easier.

## 7. You don't need a teacher, but you do need a feedback loop
- You genuinely don't need a teacher to get good. But a teacher gives you a feedback loop, and without one it's easy to go round in circles. So you have to build your own.
- When something's too hard, your brain is overloaded. Two ways to lower the load: slow down, or isolate a section.
- Same idea everywhere: in improvisation, use fewer things, one key, one set of chords, one pattern. In ear training, work out one genre, or loop just the verse until you crack it.
- Then record yourself, so you can listen back as an objective listener and hear what you couldn't feel while playing.

- **Studies show:** your working memory can only hold a few things at once, so when you overload it you make mistakes, which is exactly why slowing down and isolating work. And feedback is the single thing that drives improvement, so recording yourself is how you get it without a teacher.
